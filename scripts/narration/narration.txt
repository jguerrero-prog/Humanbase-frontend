An Interactive AI Platform for Human Biology.

Humanbase uses machine learning to trawl through decades of human genomic research data to reveal previously unseen biological connections.

From Sealfon and colleagues, Nature Methods, 2026.

Increasing quantities of genomic data present an opportunity for uncovering functional and mechanistic insights. However, given the complexity and scale of the data, sophisticated analytical frameworks and integrative approaches are required. In addition, the unique modeling requirements of different data modalities and the large number of disease states, tissue types and developmental profiles pose complex challenges. Current analysis methods are often computationally expensive and technically challenging for the end user to apply. There is a need for comprehensive resources that bridge the gap between raw data and biological insights, allowing researchers at all levels of computational fluency to bring large data compendia and cutting-edge analytical approaches to bear on their questions of interest. Here, we present Humanbase, an interactive web-based platform that uses AI to integrate and interpret large-scale human genomic data.

A user studying, for example, autism could assess whether autism spectrum disorder-linked variants affect P-TEN expression in the brain and the mechanism of dysregulation, for example, disrupted histone marks or transcription factor binding, using variant effect predictors such as Sei or ExPecto S-C. They could then trace how these effects propagate through cell-type-specific functional gene networks with module detection, revealing potential mechanisms by which genetic variants contribute to autism-related dysregulation.

Humanbase provides access to tissue- and cell-type-specific variant effects prediction frameworks and gene functional networks combined with downstream analysis tools in a user-friendly, freely available server. It integrates over 62,000 public datasets, which are pre-processed and standardized using robust pipelines. Humanbase democratizes access to extensively evaluated, computationally intensive tools via distributed computing resources, an intuitive interface requiring no coding experience, and a flexible A-P-I for advanced users. To assist users, extensive documentation, use cases for each analysis, and links to the publications describing each method are available on the platform.

Understanding the significance of noncoding variants remains a challenge. Humanbase addresses this with tissue- and cell-type-specific deep learning models trained on the human reference sequence and on large compendia that include thousands of genome-wide profiles of histone marks, transcription factor binding sites, D-N-ase hypersensitivity data and RNA-binding profiles. The Beluga and Sei sequence models predict epigenetic and regulatory impacts of variants, ExPecto and ExPecto S-C predict expression impacts of variants on tissues and cell types, and Seqweaver assesses post-transcriptional impacts of variants through RNA binding protein interactions. Each model quantifies variant impact by comparing predicted regulatory activity between reference and alternate alleles, enabling single-nucleotide-resolution predictions for any variant, including rare and previously unobserved variants. By providing the predicted impact on each chromatin and regulatory feature, the models elucidate molecular mechanisms of the effect of each variant. Predicted variant impacts are summarized with a disease impact score that quantifies the likely pathogenicity of a mutation. Additionally, an in silico mutagenesis tool allows users to simulate and visualize the effects of every possible nucleotide substitution within a sequence. Predictions can be explored interactively or downloaded for downstream analysis. These capabilities enable researchers to identify high-impact and pathogenic regulatory mutations, prioritize variants from sequencing studies and design targeted experiments.

Beyond variant analysis, Humanbase provides gene-gene functional networks for 146 tissues and cell types, for example, eosinophil, heart and renal glomerulus. The networks are constructed through Bayesian integration of 987 heterogeneous genomic datasets, spanning approximately 38,000 experimental conditions, which are weighted on the basis of accuracy and relevance in each specific cell or tissue type. Edges in the networks represent the likelihood that two genes participate in the same biological process or pathway in a particular tissue or cell type. Users can interactively explore the network neighborhoods of genes or gene sets of interest and view the experimental evidence supporting gene-gene links, for example, protein-protein interactions, co-expression, transcription factor binding or chemical genetic perturbations. Through the Net-WAS tool, the networks also provide a functional context for analysis of quantitative genetics associations, such as genome-wide association study results. Functional networks can be used to guide hypothesis generation, experimental design and data interpretation, and query results can be exported as visual images or tabular data for further use.

Through functional module detection in these context-specific networks, biomedical researchers can interpret the biology of large, complex gene sets such as those generated from screens, omics experiments or differential analyses. Users provide a gene list of 20 to 3,500 genes, and these genes are clustered into modules based on their connectivity within an automatically identified or user-selected relevant tissue network. Modules are annotated using curated databases such as G O and M Sig D B. By identifying these functionally coherent modules, this analysis reveals tissue-specific functional signals that may be overlooked by standard enrichment analyses on the entire gene set.

Between October 2024 and October 2025, Humanbase served over 20,000 users from 110 countries. It is freely accessible without registration, and its user-friendly web interface supports researchers across varying levels of computational expertise. Humanbase has supported studies addressing diverse scientific questions. By making available advanced AI models that leverage large genomic datasets, Humanbase enables the research community to perform sophisticated, context-aware, computationally expensive analyses at the level of variants, genes and gene sets.
